I was sixteen when I wrote that my first boyfriend broke up with me after I told him I hurt myself. He asked me how I could possibly love another person if I struggled to love myself. I wrote that I could not answer him then and could not answer him now. I wrote that I went home afterward and hurt myself again.
The breakup never happened. The boyfriend never existed.
I found the essay nine years later. I am twenty-five and reading it because I had relapsed, after believing for years that this was something I used to do and had left behind. I went looking for evidence of what I had felt, what I had done, who I had been before so much of my adolescence disappeared into fog.
I remember the conviction of the prose. I remember being excited to tell the story to the world. I do not remember writing about the breakup, because there was no breakup to remember.
There are true things in the essay. I began hurting myself when I was twelve. I wrote that I told my biology teacher when I was sixteen. I wrote that he hugged me. I don't know whether either happened. He has died, so I cannot ask him.
It is said that “Two can keep a secret if one of them is dead”. What about a lie?
I had gone looking for an earlier version of myself who might explain the person I had become. Instead I found a narrator who had invented a boyfriend, possibly invented a discussion with her biology teacher, and had written both with enough certainty that, nine years later, I could not separate what she remembered from what she needed the story to be.
Other people know only the versions of my life I have given them: my mother knows one, my therapists another, my friends another, my husband another still.
I once knew what I had omitted and what I had changed, which choices I'd furnished with better motives, which failures I'd made more noble, which stories I'd reorganized until the person inside them seemed worthy of sympathy, of pride, of attention. That distance felt grimy. It still does, more so now: the layers have accumulated without ever being scrubbed away, and I'm not sure they can be now, not without leaving a stain.
There is more grime, but maybe I see so much because my eyes are clearer, too. I am more self-aware—no, more self-obsessed. I look at myself whenever I pass a reflective surface. Narcissus had his pool. I am trapped in the nth recursion of a funhouse mirror facing a funhouse mirror.  Like Narcissus, I fixate on my appearance. But I also fixate on the angle, the expression, whether the expression matches what I'm feeling, whether I'm making the expression because I know I'm being watched, whether thinking it is performing.

Memory decays. Lies survive through repetition. A faulty positive feedback loop amplifies noisy signal into noisier signal. Nine years pass, and my mother mentions something I did as a teenager, and I cannot tell whether it happened or whether she remembers it because I once told her it did.
I return to my old writing to recover the truth and instead find a narrator who sounds certain, which is not the same as being honest. I used to believe the true story existed intact inside me, even if it was unspoken. Now I don't know if the true story exists at all.
But the essay exists. It contains a boyfriend who does not. And a hug that might.

Another thing that exists: a twenty-three-page neuropsychological report about me from when I was sixteen. It is the most thoroughly sourced account of my adolescence that exists. The neuropsychologist interviewed me, my parents, my school counselor, my doctors, my piano teacher, two of my teachers. She reviewed my academic records and administered tests with names like the WISC-V, the MMPI-A, the MASC-2, the BASC-2, the BRIEF, and the Rorschach.
I remember giving an answer during the Rorschach and immediately wondering whether it was my first thought or an interesting answer I'd chosen to perform.
The report knows things I don't remember, and things I do. It knows when I began having trouble in school. It knows which medication may have made my intrusive thoughts worse.
Confession: I know for a fact I did not actually have intrusive thoughts. I had hyperrealistic nightmares, possibly from melatonin, but I invented the intrusive thoughts. Eventually the lie grew into hallucinations of bugs crawling under my skin, which I used to justify a fear of bugs that began sometime after puberty and, as far as I know, has no origin story. If the only other person around to deal with a bug is more frightened than I am, I can get myself together and deal with it — which I take as evidence the fear is under my control, and therefore not real enough to exist without a story behind it.
The report knows I was hospitalized three times. It knows I struggled to make friends, that I talked "a mile a minute," that I added "multiple caveats" to my answers.
It also knows that I lied. When I was nine or ten, I wrote a note calling myself names and showed it to my parents. When they found out I'd written it myself, I said I wanted excitement and attention. In fourth grade I lied about homework I hadn't finished. Once, after a lower-than-ideal score on a test, I added a minus sign to an 8 and told my teacher she must have graded it wrong.
I don't need the report to tell me these things happened. I remember them vividly. The homework was history short-answer. US history. I lied because I hated it, and because I felt there was no point in putting in effort when my teacher didn't reward me even when I improved.
I remember I was suspended for the note on Thursday, April 7th. I wore my uniform pants into the office. I knew my best friend had already been taken in before me. I was suspended the next day, Friday; I wore a grey long-sleeved shirt with a rhinestone heart on it. On Sunday, before I went back, I wore pajamas with green pants and a peace sign, and sat on my parents' bed while they spoke to me more kindly than I deserved.
I do not remember the year. I remember the uniform pants, the rhinestone heart, the green pajamas, and my parents' kindness.
I do not remember the exact year, but I know this happened years before the biology teacher, years before the invented boyfriend. It should be deeper in the fog. It isn't.
I believe this memory. That is almost more frightening.
Time and my generally poor memory cannot explain this. Maybe depression obscured some things. Maybe lies obscured others. Maybe repetition preserved whichever version I told most often. Maybe shame burned certain events into place. Maybe memory has no obligation to follow a rule I can understand.
—
The report diagnoses me with ADHD, anxiety, and major depressive disorder. It attributes the irritability, racing thoughts, elevated mood, and excessive speech to ADHD and anxiety. It notes emotional lability, recommends considering a mood stabilizer, and does not diagnose bipolar disorder.
Nine years later, I am convinced I have bipolar II. I don't believe I have ADHD.
My mother and I call what I get "fits of despair." They arrive abruptly, last a week or a month, and reorganize every fact around the premise that I am worthless. A rejection that would ordinarily hurt and pass becomes proof I've always been worthless and everyone already knows it.
I studied math. I should know better than to trust a proof whose axiom appeared without justification. Knowing that doesn't make the proof feel invalid.
I want bipolar II to be the answer because it would make the switches, the racing thoughts, the despair cohere, and would make them feel less like personal failures. But wanting an explanation because it would make my experience coherent doesn't make the explanation true — and I am not just evaluating the model; I am part of the evidence, and I selected which to consider for the model, and I want one model to win.
I had imagined this as an essay about discovering bipolar II during my PhD. That would have been a good story. But I do not have the diagnosis. The people whose job it is to diagnose me do not agree. My husband doesn't agree. My parents don't agree.
Racing thoughts can belong to ADHD, anxiety, hypomania, or a person who simply thinks quickly. Emotional lability can become evidence for whichever explanation you already prefer. I arrange the facts into bipolar II. The neuropsychologist arranged many of the same facts into ADHD, anxiety, depression. People around me arrange them into "my personality," or "my intensity" — a word that has always felt like a euphemism for something worse when applied to me.
I love coherence. It's why I like math, and philosophy — starting with a few premises and discovering what follows, or recognizing a structure shared by two unrelated problems.
In mathematics, internal consistency gets you somewhere. In my life, I don't know whether the premises are true. The evidence is incomplete. The witnesses disagree. One of the witnesses is me.
Given enough facts, I can build a model that accounts for all of them. Given contradictory facts, I can add caveats to preserve it. Given a missing fact, I can infer what belongs in the gap. Given a gap I need filled badly enough, I can invent a boyfriend, a hug, intrusive thoughts, hallucinations of bugs. I can invent something worse that I'm not prepared to describe here and build justifications on top of it, and justifications on top of those. And I don’t know why I still do it.
A model can explain every observation available to it and still be wrong, especially when the person building it also chose which observations counted.
—
Another thing that exists: an essay I wrote at fifteen, about limoncello in Italy.
“It was a shot of limoncello after dinner, potent and sour — affecting me less than I wanted it to, so I faked it. […] I wanted to be light and airy and euphoric when I was really just sleepy and content. […] All around me I heard glasses clinking, people laughing, and I wondered if they were pretending the way I was.”
The feeling failed to fit the model, so I made myself fit it, then treated my performance as evidence about everyone else. For the record, alcohol does work. I know that now. The altered state is real.
At fifteen I wondered whether everyone else was pretending. At twenty-five I wonder why I feel I have to pretend at all.
—
I thought I would write this about hurting myself again. Then I thought I would write about my PhD experience and a diagnosis I don't have. Then I thought I was writing about lying, or memory, or the gap between an experience and the story required to justify it. I realize I am writing about a model of my relationship to the truth — which is itself the kind of thing I am good at building and cannot trust.
I selected the evidence: the old essays, the report, the rhinestone heart, the limoncello, the boyfriend who never existed. I decided which details were signal and which were noise. I wrote the transitions that made unrelated things feel connected.
I wanted to put something true somewhere outside myself — not where I could revise it forever, but here, where it can exist without anyone I know needing to see it.
But this cannot be the true story of my life. I do not possess that story. I have memories I trust, memories I doubt, lies I remember telling, lies I no longer remember inventing, and at least one truth I am still too afraid to tell.
This is not a complete confession. It may not even be a reliable one.
What I can do is mark the places where I don't know.
What I want to do is stop filling them in.
